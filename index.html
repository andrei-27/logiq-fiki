02 NOVEMBER 2026 · VALIDATION 1 (IDEA)

M1 framed the problem as a hypothesis: online content mixes claims with persuasive framing, which can make evaluation effortful. We hypothesize that a brief, contextual explanation and a question about missing evidence could help readers assess arguments while reading. Our first recorded exploration is based on two Romanian interviews: Gabi, a legal advisor, and Baldo, a recent computer science graduate working as a software engineer. LogiQ was not presented, so the solution remains untested.

Interview questions

These are the eight customer-discovery questions provided for M2. The interviews were conducted in Romanian; the linked transcripts include the original dialogue and English translations.

  1. Think about the last time you encountered an online claim or argument that you wanted to evaluate. What happened?
  2. What were you trying to accomplish in that situation, and why did the argument matter to you?
  3. How did you decide whether the argument was convincing? Walk me through what you actually did.
  4. What, if anything, was difficult, time-consuming, or left unresolved during that process?
  5. Can you recall an occasion when you decided not to investigate an online argument? What made you move on?
  6. What tools, resources, or people have you turned to when trying to understand an argument? Tell me about the last time you used them.
  7. Do you know anyone, especially someone close to you, who has recently believed fake news or internet propaganda and used it to try to win an argument?
  8. Do you think it’s hard to spot fake news in the new era, especially in the last few years, when creating pieces of misinformation with AI help has become easier than ever?

Findings: what each participant said

Short quotes are English translations of the Romanian transcript. Timestamps link to the transcript.

Gabi

  • Described evaluating news as something debated “at the office with my colleagues” that “happens daily”. Gabi did not describe a specific recent case. 00:12
  • Judges a story by its source: the person it comes from, whether they have “a good reputation or a bad reputation”, and how much Gabi trusts the newspaper or TV channel. 00:57, 01:36
  • Compares outlets: if several websites or channels present a story the same way, “it means it’s OK”; if they differ, “something doesn’t add up”. 02:43
  • Said people with strong doctrinal or political beliefs call contradicting information false, and that Gabi has “no reason to bother” trying to persuade them. 03:36, 04:12
  • Said AI-made stories are “hard to counter” because AI “knows how to formulate fairly plausible arguments”, calling them “a real danger”. 05:02
  • On the interviewer’s get-rich-quick example, Gabi pointed to its implausibility: overnight wealth, access limited to “the bank people”, and a sign-up website. Gabi also said “a fairly large percentage of the population” cannot tell false stories from true ones. 06:26
  • Asked about solutions, Gabi proposed civic education starting in school or kindergarten. For adults, Gabi proposed mass-media programmes, especially on public TV or through state partnerships with high-rated channels. Gabi did not mention an app or tool, including after the interviewer suggested “an app or a course”. 08:07, 09:43

Baldo

  • Most recent case: a fact concerning “a personal investment”. Baldo searched Google and checked “two or three websites” for “a credible source behind it”. 00:13, 00:32, 01:06
  • Found it convincing because an official institution published it. Baldo attributed the document to the “FCC” but was unsure what the abbreviation stands for. 01:24
  • Checking is time-consuming when a claim is not “binary”, because it needs several sources and opinions from various people. Binary claims are “much easier”. 02:20
  • Skipped investigating when a claim “wasn’t that important”. 03:21
  • Tools: community notes on X/Twitter, and asking “an AI agent” for sources “both for and against”. For very important claims Baldo checks each source in more detail; otherwise a simple search, “AI-assisted or manual”, may be enough. 03:41 (This passage comes from one speech model only.)
  • Said “pretty much everyone” is vulnerable, because people are less likely to check something that fits what they already believe. Baldo gave a hypothetical political example. 05:48, 06:30
  • Said spotting misinformation has become harder in the AI era. Tells for fake images and videos stop working as generators improve; for text, finding sources is “a better sign … but it isn’t 100%”. Baldo has not personally encountered voice deepfakes. 07:22, 07:45

Interpretation: exploratory themes

These are early observations from two interviews, not representative findings. The machine-assisted transcripts and translations have not been checked by a human listener.

  • Checking centred on sources, not reasoning. Both participants judged claims by who published them and whether other sources agree. Neither described analysing an argument’s reasoning or mentioned the logical flaws and persuasion techniques LogiQ is designed around. The questions did not probe this directly, so this does not show the reasoning problem is absent. It does weaken the assumption that readers already think about arguments in those terms.
  • Effort followed the stakes. Baldo checked a claim tied to a personal investment and skipped unimportant ones. If this pattern holds, help may be wanted mainly when a claim feels consequential and ignored otherwise.
  • The clearest pain was time. Baldo described the cost of checking claims that are not clear-cut. Gabi described difficulty mainly for other people.
  • Alternatives are already in use: search engines, comparing outlets, community notes and a general AI agent. LogiQ would need a clear advantage over these, especially over general-purpose AI assistants.
  • Vulnerability was placed in others. Gabi pointed to people with strong beliefs; Baldo said “pretty much everyone”. Together with Gabi’s preference for school and media education, this hints that the person who sees the problem may not be the one who would use a tool. That fits M1’s idea of educators and media-literacy teams as possible buyers, but it is speculative.
  • AI-made misinformation raised concern: both participants said it was harder to assess, but this is an opinion rather than an observed behavior.

Effect on the product and its assumptions

  • “Checking a claim takes effort”: partly supported, by one participant, for claims that are not clear-cut.
  • “Readers want explanations of reasoning while reading”: not supported and not directly tested. This remains the main assumption to test. The next interviews should ask how people judge an argument when its source looks credible.
  • Possible adjustment to test, not yet decided: both participants relied on sources and corroboration. LogiQ’s “ask a better question” step could point to the missing evidence or sources, not only label the reasoning.
  • Target segment: the evidence does not support changing it. These two professional backgrounds do not establish fit with “students & curious readers”, and two interviews cannot establish a segment. Potential contexts to explore include people judging high-stakes claims and educators.
  • Product: no change is justified yet. The concept stays as described, with the points above recorded as assumptions to test.

Validation decision

Not ready for Customer Validation (testing selling).

  • Only two interviews, with recruitment and backgrounds undocumented, so no customer segment has been identified.
  • The problem as LogiQ frames it, difficulty judging reasoning, was not clearly observed. The pain that did appear, the time needed for claims that are not clear-cut, came from one participant.
  • Both participants already use free alternatives. Neither expressed a need for a tool like LogiQ, and Gabi proposed education instead.
  • The solution and willingness to pay were not tested.
  • Some answers were shaped by leading or solution-oriented questions.

Next: check the transcripts against the audio and interview more people in a clearly defined segment about their past behavior before testing an offer.

02 / THE PROBLEM & THE IDEA

More information.
Less room to reflect.

Online, a confident claim can feel like a convincing argument. They are not always the same thing.

Headlines, posts, and comment threads mix facts with emotional appeals, personal attacks, and unsupported conclusions. Checking a claim takes effort; understanding how it tries to persuade us takes another kind of attention.

Our starting hypothesis: short explanations, offered in context, can help people evaluate arguments without interrupting the way they read.

↳

The question we’re exploring
Can a small moment of reflection become a lasting critical-thinking habit?

OUR VALUE PROPOSITION

A second look.
Not the last word.

LogiQ is designed to explain what may be problematic in an argument, so the reader can decide what to think.

01 — NOTICE

Spot the reasoning.

Highlight a passage that may contain a logical flaw or a persuasive technique. Start with selected text; explore contextual browser support next.

02 — UNDERSTAND

Explain the leap.

Show a short, plain-language explanation tied to the exact words. Offer more detail only when the reader wants it.

03 — REFLECT

Ask a better question.

Suggest what evidence or context is missing. Invite the reader to challenge the analysis and give feedback.

THE ARGUMENT

“Thousands of people shared this claim, so it must be true.”
QPotential appeal to popularity

A claim’s popularity tells us how widely it has spread, not whether the evidence supports it. People can share something for many reasons.

ASK YOURSELF

What evidence supports the claim beyond the number of shares?

A little more context +

Popularity can tell us what people believe. It becomes a reasoning problem when it is used as sufficient proof that a factual claim is true. The claim might still be true; this argument does not establish it.

THE PRODUCT WE WANT TO BUILD

↗

Explain, don’t dictate.

Make the reasoning visible and leave the conclusion with the reader.

≈

Leave room for nuance.

Flag uncertainty. Strong emotion alone does not make an argument invalid.

◎

Earn the interruption.

Prefer a few useful insights over a feed full of warnings.

OUR ADVANTAGE HYPOTHESIS

Useful explanations at the moment of reading can make critical thinking easier to practice. Clear language, contextual delivery, and progressive detail are our proposed differentiators.
