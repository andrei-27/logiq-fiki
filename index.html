02 NOVEMBER 2026 · VALIDATION 1 (IDEA)

M1 framed the problem as a hypothesis: online content mixes claims with persuasive framing, which can make evaluation effortful. We hypothesize that a brief, contextual explanation and a question about missing evidence could help readers assess arguments while reading.

To test the problem, we talk to people through recorded interviews, chat interviews and an online survey. Their answers are on the Interviews and Findings pages, and new survey responses appear there automatically once their authors consent to it. Most conversations focus on the problem rather than on LogiQ itself, so the solution is still largely untested.

Open the online survey ↗Browse interviews and responses →Findings: what each participant said →

What each participant said is on the findings page. The sections below are our interpretation, and we revise them as new interviews and survey responses come in.

Interview questions

These are the customer-discovery questions we use for M2 interviews and the survey. Interviews are held in the participant’s language; transcripts include the original dialogue and an English translation where needed.

  1. Think about the last time you encountered an online claim or argument that you wanted to evaluate. What happened?
  2. What were you trying to accomplish in that situation, and why did the argument matter to you?
  3. How did you decide whether the argument was convincing? Walk me through what you actually did.
  4. What, if anything, was difficult, time-consuming, or left unresolved during that process?
  5. Can you recall an occasion when you decided not to investigate an online argument? What made you move on?
  6. What tools, resources, or people have you turned to when trying to understand an argument? Tell me about the last time you used them.
  7. Do you know anyone, especially someone close to you, who has recently believed fake news or internet propaganda and used it to try to win an argument?
  8. Do you think it’s hard to spot fake news in the new era, especially in the last few years, when creating pieces of misinformation with AI help has become easier than ever?

What we’re learning

Early patterns from the conversations so far, not representative results.

  • People check sources, not reasoning. When a claim seems doubtful, participants search for it, compare how different outlets report it, or look for a credible, independent source. So far nobody has described examining how an argument reasons, which is the step LogiQ is built around. Our questions haven’t asked about this directly yet, so we can’t rule it out.
  • Effort depends on what’s at stake. People dig in when a claim affects them, such as an investment decision, and let it go when the topic feels unimportant or something else takes their attention.
  • The hard part is often other people. Checking a claim for yourself seems manageable, although claims that aren’t clear-cut take longer. Conversations kept turning, partly prompted by our questions, to friends or relatives who believe something false and are hard to persuade.
  • Trust in AI cuts both ways. Some participants already use AI assistants to gather sources; others avoid AI-generated content or see it as a source of misinformation. A tool like LogiQ would need to show its reasoning rather than ask to be trusted.
  • LogiQ’s purpose has to be obvious. Where the idea came up, the reaction was curious but cautious: interesting if done well, but not clear enough about what it does.

Effect on the product and its assumptions

Our core assumption, that readers want help understanding how an argument works while they read, is still open. What we have heard so far points to a few changes worth testing in the M3 prototype:

  • Start from the evidence. People already check sources, so an explanation could point to the missing evidence or a source worth checking, not only name a reasoning flaw.
  • Keep it effortless. People skip checks that feel unimportant, so LogiQ should be quick to use, easy to ignore, and most helpful when a claim matters to the reader.
  • Make it easy to share. A short, plain explanation could help someone talk a friend or relative through why a claim is misleading.
  • Explain, don’t judge. Show why a passage is flagged instead of giving an AI verdict, so people who distrust AI can follow the reasoning themselves.
  • Say what LogiQ is in one sentence. The landing page should make its purpose clear at a glance.

We are not changing the target segment yet. The people we have spoken to don’t yet show whether “students & curious readers” is the right first audience. Groups worth exploring include people judging high-stakes claims such as personal finance, people trying to correct friends or family, and educators.

Validation decision

Not ready for Customer Validation (testing selling) yet. Our conversations show that people do run into questionable claims online, but not yet that the problem LogiQ addresses feels urgent enough to pay for a solution:

  • The pains we hear about most are time and persuading others, not difficulty judging an argument’s reasoning.
  • People often let questionable claims go, which suggests the problem may not feel urgent.
  • Free tools already cover much of what people do today: search engines, community notes and AI assistants.
  • We haven’t tested the solution or willingness to pay, and we haven’t documented how participants were recruited, so we can’t yet name a clear customer segment.
  • Some of our interview questions were leading, which weakens the evidence.

What would change this decision: consistent evidence, from a clearly defined group, that judging arguments is a real and recurring difficulty, plus a positive response to the M3 prototype.

Next: keep reviewing interviews and survey responses as they arrive, check the transcripts against the audio, define one segment and document how we recruit, and use the M3 prototype and landing page to test whether people understand and want LogiQ.

02 / THE PROBLEM & THE IDEA

More information.
Less room to reflect.

Online, a confident claim can feel like a convincing argument. They are not always the same thing.

Headlines, posts, and comment threads mix facts with emotional appeals, personal attacks, and unsupported conclusions. Checking a claim takes effort; understanding how it tries to persuade us takes another kind of attention.

Our starting hypothesis: short explanations, offered in context, can help people evaluate arguments without interrupting the way they read.

↳

The question we’re exploring
Can a small moment of reflection become a lasting critical-thinking habit?

OUR VALUE PROPOSITION

A second look.
Not the last word.

LogiQ is designed to explain what may be problematic in an argument, so the reader can decide what to think.

01 — NOTICE

Spot the reasoning.

Highlight a passage that may contain a logical flaw or a persuasive technique. Start with selected text; explore contextual browser support next.

02 — UNDERSTAND

Explain the leap.

Show a short, plain-language explanation tied to the exact words. Offer more detail only when the reader wants it.

03 — REFLECT

Ask a better question.

Suggest what evidence or context is missing. Invite the reader to challenge the analysis and give feedback.

THE ARGUMENT

“Thousands of people shared this claim, so it must be true.”
QPotential appeal to popularity

A claim’s popularity tells us how widely it has spread, not whether the evidence supports it. People can share something for many reasons.

ASK YOURSELF

What evidence supports the claim beyond the number of shares?

A little more context +

Popularity can tell us what people believe. It becomes a reasoning problem when it is used as sufficient proof that a factual claim is true. The claim might still be true; this argument does not establish it.

THE PRODUCT WE WANT TO BUILD

↗

Explain, don’t dictate.

Make the reasoning visible and leave the conclusion with the reader.

≈

Leave room for nuance.

Flag uncertainty. Strong emotion alone does not make an argument invalid.

◎

Earn the interruption.

Prefer a few useful insights over a feed full of warnings.

OUR ADVANTAGE HYPOTHESIS

Useful explanations at the moment of reading can make critical thinking easier to practice. Clear language, contextual delivery, and progressive detail are our proposed differentiators.
