Gabi

  • Described evaluating news as something debated “at the office with my colleagues” that “happens daily”. Gabi did not describe a specific recent case. 00:12
  • Judges a story by its source: the person it comes from, whether they have “a good reputation or a bad reputation”, and how much Gabi trusts the newspaper or TV channel. 00:57, 01:36
  • Compares outlets: if several websites or channels present a story the same way, “it means it’s OK”; if they differ, “something doesn’t add up”. 02:43

For adults, Gabi proposed mass-media programmes, especially on public TV or through state partnerships with high-rated channels. Gabi did not mention an app or tool, including after the interviewer suggested “an app or a course”. 08:07, 09:43

Baldo

  • Most recent case: a fact concerning “a personal investment”. Baldo searched Google and checked “two or three websites” for “a credible source behind it”. 00:13, 00:32, 01:06
  • Found it convincing because an official institution published it. Baldo attributed the document to the “FCC” but was unsure what the abbreviation stands for. 01:24
  • Checking is time-consuming when a claim is not “binary”, because it needs several sources and opinions from various people. Binary claims are “much easier”. 02:20
  • Skipped investigating when a claim “wasn’t that important”. 03:21

  • Said spotting misinformation has become harder in the AI era. Tells for fake images and videos stop working as generators improve; for text, finding sources is “a better sign … but it isn’t 100%”. Baldo has not personally encountered voice deepfakes. 07:22, 07:45

Georgiana

  • Most recent case, about a week before the interview: news about measures taken over strikes in France. Georgiana wanted to know whether the source was official, and checked official websites as well as news sites. 00:21, 00:37
  • It mattered because Georgiana lives there now and needed to know whether the measures applied to them. 01:04
  • The hard part was understanding: some information was incomplete, some wording was unfamiliar, and stories pointed to “a lot of references” through links instead of explaining the whole point. 01:31
  • Studies the energy sector and notices stories in that field that “aren’t written correctly in terms of the words they use”, as well as “far more exaggerated headlines to grab attention”. 02:24
  • Doesn’t look further when a subject isn’t of particular interest or when Georgiana already knows the correct information. 02:24

  • In general groups with colleagues, stories get forwarded, but Georgiana doesn’t think people were completely fooled: they mostly sent them to ask the others’ opinion. Georgiana has seen people who are fooled, though not in their close circle. 04:36
  • Said it is getting harder: generated images “can be used as false proof” and are of good quality, so publications need to check images more thoroughly. Responsibility also lies with people checking sources, and possibly with stricter legislation and penalties for fake news. 05:46

RESEARCH INTERPRETATION

What this means for LogiQ

Interview questions

These are the customer-discovery questions we use for M2 interviews and the survey. Interviews are held in the participant’s language; transcripts include the original dialogue and an English translation where needed.

  1. Think about the last time you encountered an online claim or argument that you wanted to evaluate. What happened?
  2. What were you trying to accomplish in that situation, and why did the argument matter to you?
  3. How did you decide whether the argument was convincing? Walk me through what you actually did.
  4. What, if anything, was difficult, time-consuming, or left unresolved during that process?
  5. Can you recall an occasion when you decided not to investigate an online argument? What made you move on?
  6. What tools, resources, or people have you turned to when trying to understand an argument? Tell me about the last time you used them.
  7. Do you know anyone, especially someone close to you, who has recently believed fake news or internet propaganda and used it to try to win an argument?
  8. Do you think it’s hard to spot fake news in the new era, especially in the last few years, when creating pieces of misinformation with AI help has become easier than ever?

What we’re learning

Early patterns from the conversations so far, not representative results.

  • People check sources, not reasoning. When a claim seems doubtful, participants search for it, compare how different outlets report it, or look for a credible, independent source. So far nobody has described examining how an argument reasons, which is one aspect of critical thinking; the closest was noticing exaggerated headlines and imprecise wording in a familiar field. Our questions haven’t asked about this directly yet, so we can’t rule it out.
  • Understanding can be the hard part. Incomplete information, unfamiliar wording, and stories that point to references instead of explaining them can leave people unsure, even when the source is official.
  • Effort depends on what’s at stake. People dig in when a claim affects them, such as an investment decision, and let it go when the topic feels unimportant or something else takes their attention.
  • The hard part is often other people. Checking a claim for yourself seems manageable, although claims that aren’t clear-cut take longer. Conversations kept turning, partly prompted by our questions, to friends or relatives who believe something false and are hard to persuade.
  • Trust in AI cuts both ways. Some participants already use AI assistants to gather sources; others avoid AI-generated content or see it as a source of misinformation. Some also see a role for publishers and stricter rules, not only for individual readers. A tool like LogiQ would need to show its reasoning rather than ask to be trusted.
  • LogiQ’s purpose has to be obvious. Where the idea came up, the reaction was curious but cautious: interesting if done well, but not clear enough about what it does.

Effect on the product and its assumptions

Our core assumption, that readers want help understanding how an argument works while they read, is still open. What we have heard so far points to a few changes worth testing in the LogiQ prototype:

  • Start from the evidence. People already check sources, so an explanation could point to the missing evidence or a source worth checking, not only name a reasoning flaw.
  • Clarify the wording. Explain unfamiliar terms and summarise what a referenced source actually says, since unclear wording can block understanding before reasoning even comes into play.
  • Keep it effortless. People skip checks that feel unimportant, so LogiQ should be quick to use, easy to ignore, and most helpful when a claim matters to the reader.
  • Support conversations with evidence. Original sources and plain explanations may help someone discuss a claim with a friend or relative. Sharing tools are outside the current prototype.
  • Explain, don’t judge. Show why a passage is flagged instead of giving an AI verdict, so people who distrust AI can follow the reasoning themselves.
  • Say what LogiQ is in one sentence. The landing page should make its purpose clear at a glance.

We are not changing the target segment yet. The people we have spoken to don’t yet show whether “students & curious readers” is the right first audience. Groups worth exploring include people judging high-stakes claims such as personal finance, people trying to correct friends or family, and educators.

Validation decision

Not ready for Customer Validation (testing selling) yet. Our conversations show that people do run into questionable claims online, but not yet that the problem LogiQ addresses feels urgent enough to pay for a solution:

  • The pains we hear about most are time, unclear or incomplete information, and persuading others, rather than difficulty judging an argument’s reasoning.
  • People often let questionable claims go, which suggests the problem may not feel urgent.
  • Free tools already cover much of what people do today: search engines, community notes and AI assistants.
  • We haven’t tested the solution or willingness to pay, and we haven’t documented how participants were recruited, so we can’t yet name a clear customer segment.
  • Some of our interview questions were leading, which weakens the evidence.

What would change this decision: consistent evidence, from a clearly defined group, that checking news claims is a real and recurring difficulty, plus a positive response to the LogiQ prototype.

Next: keep reviewing interviews and survey responses as they arrive, check the transcripts against the audio, define one segment and document how we recruit, and use the LogiQ prototype and landing page to test whether people understand and want LogiQ.
